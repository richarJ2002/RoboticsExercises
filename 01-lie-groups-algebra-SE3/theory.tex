\Section{Introduction}

This document covers the theory behind Lie Groups, Rotations and
Transformations. This serves as an introductory course to the maths and shows
examples of workflows and implementation.

\Section{Coordinate Frames}

Suppose we have 2 coordinate frames, frame A and frame B. Let's say we can
describe the rotation from frame $A$ to frame $B$ through a rotation matrix,
${}^B\mathbf{R}_A \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$ and a translation vector
$^B\mathbf{t}_A \in \mathbb{R}^3$. And let's say we have a point in space
described in frame $A$, ${}^A\mathbf{p}$. We can then transalte the point into frame $B$,
${}^B\mathbf{p}$, via:

\begin{equation}
    {}^B\mathbf{p}
    =
    {}^B\mathbf{R}_A {}^A\mathbf{p}
    +
    {}^B\mathbf{t}_A
    \label{eq:point-transformation}
\end{equation}

First thing to notice is that a rigid body transformation is both a rotation and
a translation. The definition of the variables in \ref{eq:point-transformation}
are as follows:

\vspace{0.5cm}

\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll}
    \hspace{5cm}${}^B\mathbf{p}$   & \hspace{0.1cm} : \hspace{0.1cm} Point expressed in frame $B$.                                            \\
    \hspace{5cm}${}^B\mathbf{R}_A$ & \hspace{0.1cm} : \hspace{0.1cm} Rotation matrix from frame $A$ to frame $B$.                             \\
    \hspace{5cm}${}^A\mathbf{p}$   & \hspace{0.1cm} : \hspace{0.1cm} Point expressed in frame $A$.                                            \\
    \hspace{5cm}${}^B\mathbf{t}_A$ & \hspace{0.1cm} : \hspace{0.1cm} Translation from frame $A$ to frame $B$ \textbf{expressed} in frame $B$.
\end{tabular}

\vspace{0.5cm}

It is important to get a clear understanding of what the variables mean and what
frames they are in. Figure \ref{fig:coordinate-frames} shows a diagram of the
different vectors.

\vspace{0.3cm}

\begin{figure}[ht]

    \caption{Figure showing the transformation of points between two coordinate frames $A$ and $B$.}
    \label{fig:coordinate-frames}
\end{figure}

\Section{SO(3): Rotation}

A 3D point can be expressed by a $3\times3$ rotation matrix:

\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{R} = \begin{bmatrix}
        r_{11} & r_{12} & r_{13} \\
        r_{21} & r_{22} & r_{23} \\
        r_{31} & r_{32} & r_{33} \\
    \end{bmatrix}
    \label{eq:rotation-matrix}
\end{equation}
\vspace{0.3cm}

A valid rotation matrix must satisfy the following constraints:

\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^T = \mathbf{R}^T\mathbf{R} = \mathbb{I}_{3\times3}
    \label{eq:rotation-matrix-transpose-constraint}
\end{equation}
\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{equation}
    \det(\mathbf{R}) = 1
    \label{eq:rotation-matrix-determinant-constraint}
\end{equation}
\vspace{0.3cm}

All matricies which satisfy the constraints
\ref{eq:rotation-matrix-transpose-constraint} and
\ref{eq:rotation-matrix-determinant-constraint} are denoted as
\textbf{Special Orthogonal} (\(SO\)) matricies. With \(SO\)(3) denoting a 3
dimensional rotational space. This can be written as:

\begin{equation}
    SO(3) =
    \left\{
    \mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}
    :
    \hspace{0.2cm}
    \mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^T = \mathbb{I}_{3\times3},
    \hspace{0.2cm}
    \det(\mathbf{R}) = 1
    \right\}
    \label{eq:so3-definition}
\end{equation}

\Subsection{Why is SO(3) a group?}

The special orthogonal group $SO(3)$ is a group because the composition of
rotation matrices satisfies the four group properties: closure, identity,
inverse, and associativity.

\vspace{1cm}

\Property{Composition}

The composition of two rotation matrices produces another valid rotation
matrix. Therefore, for $\mathbf{R}_1,\mathbf{R}_2 \in SO(3)$:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{R}_3
    =
    \mathbf{R}_2\mathbf{R}_1
    \in SO(3)
\end{equation}

\Property{Identity}

There exists an identity rotation that leaves every vector unchanged. This is
represented by the identity matrix $\mathbb{I}_{3\times3}$:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{R}\mathbf{I}
    =
    \mathbf{I}\mathbf{R}
    =
    \mathbf{R}
\end{equation}

\Property{Inverse}

Every rotation can be undone by applying its inverse. For rotation matrices,
the inverse is equal to the transpose:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{R}^{-1}
    =
    \mathbf{R}^{T}
\end{equation}

\Property{Associativity}

The grouping of multiple rotations does not affect the resulting rotation:

\begin{equation}
    \left(\mathbf{R}_1\mathbf{R}_2\right)\mathbf{R}_3
    =
    \mathbf{R}_1\left(\mathbf{R}_2\mathbf{R}_3\right)
\end{equation}

\vspace{1cm}

Therefore, $SO(3)$ forms a group. The important additional property is that
$SO(3)$ is also a smooth mathematical manifold. This is what makes $SO(3)$ a
Lie group. A general $3\times3$ matrix has 9 independent elements. However, a rotation
matrix is constrained by the orthogonality and determinant conditions defined
above. These constraints reduce the number of independent degrees of freedom
to 3. This structure allows rotations to be represented locally using a
corresponding Lie algebra.

\Subsubsection{Axis-Angle Representation}

Every 3D rotationn can be described with:
\begin{description}
    \item \hspace{2cm}- An axis of rotation $\vec{u}$ where, $\vec{u} = 1$.
    \item \hspace{2cm}- An angle of rotation $\theta$, measured in radians.
\end{description}

\vspace{0.5cm}

We can combine these into a single vector called the rotation vector
$\vec{\phi}$:

\begin{equation}
    \vec{\phi} = \theta \vec{u}
    \label{eq:rotation-vector-definition}
\end{equation}

which has the properties:
\begin{description}
    \item \hspace{2cm}- $|\vec{\phi}| = \theta$
    \item \hspace{2cm}- $\frac{\vec{\phi}}{\theta} = \vec{u}$
\end{description}

\vspace{0.3cm}

Hence, a 3D rotation can be described by a 3D vector.

\Subsubsection{The Hat Operator}

Given:

\begin{equation}
    \vec{\phi} = \begin{bmatrix}
        \phi_x \\
        \phi_y \\
        \phi_z
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

we can define:

\begin{equation}
    \text{\textasciicircum} : \mathbb{R}^3 \rightarrow \mathcal{SO}(3)
\end{equation}

as:

\begin{equation}
    \vec{\phi}\text{\textasciicircum} = \begin{bmatrix}
        0       & -\phi_z & \phi_y  \\
        -\phi_z & 0       & -\phi_x \\
        -\phi_y & \phi_x  & 0       \\
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

This matrix has the important property:

\vspace{0.1cm}
\begin{equation}
    \vec{\phi}\text{\textasciicircum}\vec{x} = \vec{\phi} \times \vec{x}
\end{equation}
\vspace{0.1cm}

The set of all matricies of the form $\vec{\phi}\text{\textasciicircum}$ is
called $\mathcal{SO}(3)$.

\vspace{0.1cm}
\begin{description}
    \item \hspace{2cm}$\text{SO(3)}$ \hspace{0.1cm} : \hspace{0.1cm} Contains actual rotation
    \item \hspace{2cm}$\mathcal{SO}\text{(3)}$ \hspace{0.1cm} : \hspace{0.1cm} Contains infitesmal rotations / rotation
          vector represented as matricies.
\end{description}
\vspace{0.3cm}

The exponential and logarithmic map connect the two together.

\Subsection{The Exponential Map}

Suppose we have a rotation vector, $\vec{\phi}$. We can use the exponential
map to transform it into a rotation matrix:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{R} = \exp\left(\vec{\phi}\text{\textasciicircum}\right)
\end{equation}

This is the Lie group exponential map:

\begin{equation}
    \exp : \mathcal{SO}(3) \rightarrow SO(3)
\end{equation}

The exponential map can be defined using the matrix exponential:

\begin{equation}
    \exp\left(\vec{\phi}\text{\textasciicircum}\right)
    =
    \mathbf{I}_{3\times3}
    +
    \vec{\phi}\text{\textasciicircum}
    +
    \frac{1}{2!}
    \left(\vec{\phi}\text{\textasciicircum}\right)^2
    +
    \frac{1}{3!}
    \left(\vec{\phi}\text{\textasciicircum}\right)^3
    + \cdots
\end{equation}

This infinite series has a closed-form solution called the
Rodrigues rotation formula.

\begin{equation}
    \theta = \left\|\vec{\phi}\right\|
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{R} =
    \mathbb{I}_{3\times3}
    +
    \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}
    \vec{\phi}\text{\textasciicircum}
    +
    \frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2}
    \left(\vec{\phi}\text{\textasciicircum}\right)^2
\end{equation}

This converts:

\begin{equation}
    \vec{\phi} \rightarrow \vec{\phi}\text{\textasciicircum} \rightarrow \mathbf{R}
\end{equation}

\Subsection{The Logarithmic Map}

We can go the other way as well. Suppose we have a rotation matrix,
$\mathbf{R}$. We can use the logarithmic map to transform it into a rotation
vector:

\begin{equation}
    \vec{\phi} = \log\left(\mathbf{R}\right)
\end{equation}

This is the Lie group logarithmic map:

\begin{equation}
    \log : \text{SO}(3) \rightarrow \mathcal{SO}(3)
\end{equation}

The logarithmic map can be defined using the following:

\begin{align}
    \theta = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{R})-1}{2}\right) \\
    \vec{\phi} = \frac{\theta}{2\sin{\theta}}\left(\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{R}^T\right)
\end{align}

\Section{SE(3): Translation}

Now we can add translation:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{T} = \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf{R} & \vec{t} \\
        0          & 1
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Where:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{R} & \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3} \\
    \vec{t}    & \in \mathbb{R}^3
\end{align}

The set of all transformation is $\text{SE}(3)$. Formally it is:

\begin{equation}
    \text{SE}(3) = \left\{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R} & \vec{t} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} : \mathbf{R} \in \text{SO}(3), \vec{t} \in \mathbb{R}^3\right\}
\end{equation}

This is the group of 3D rigid-body transformations.

\Subsection{Homogeneous Coordinates}

Normally:

\begin{equation}
    {}^B\vec{p} = {}^B\mathbf{R}_A{}^A\vec{p} + {}^B\vec{t}_A
\end{equation}

The rotation and transaltion are seperate. Using homogeneous coordinates:

\begin{equation}
    \tilde{p} = \begin{bmatrix}
        \vec{p} \\
        1
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Then we can write:

\begin{equation}
    {}^B\tilde{p} = {}^B\mathbf{T}_A {}^A\tilde{p}
\end{equation}

This then allows:

\begin{equation}
    {}^C\mathbf{T}_A = {}^C\mathbf{T}_B {}^B\mathbf{T}_A
\end{equation}

\Subsection{SE(3) Inverse}

If:

\begin{equation}
    {}^B\mathbf{T}_A = \begin{bmatrix}
        {}^B\mathbf{R}_A & {}^B\vec{t}_A \\
        0                & 1
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Then the inverse is:

\begin{equation}
    {}^B\mathbf{T}_A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix}
        {}^B\mathbf{R}_A{}^T & -{}^B\mathbf{R}_A{}^T{}^B\vec{t}_A \\
        0                    & 1
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Notice that the translation is not simply $-{}^B\vec{t}_A$. It must first be
rotated.
